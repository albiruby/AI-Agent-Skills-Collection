# Subskill 19 — Hand-Object Interaction

## Purpose
Treat the hand as the highest-risk part of the frame. Plan hand pose, grip, and framing so contact is clear and anatomy stays stable.

## Anatomy constraints to state or protect
- Five fingers per human hand (thumb plus four), each with consistent length order. Stylized characters: state the exact count (for example, three fingers and a thumb) and keep it.
- Fingers bend toward the palm; joints do not bend backward.
- The thumb opposes the fingers in grips.
- Wrist rotation has limits; the forearm may rotate (pronation/supination) to align the palm.
- Left/right: specify anatomical left/right from the **character's body**, not the viewer's screen (see subskill 04).
- Keep hand size proportional to the object and body.

## Grip taxonomy (choose per object)
| Grip | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Power grip | Cylindrical handles (watering can top handle, mug, bat) | Fingers wrap around, thumb wraps or rests on top |
| Hook grip | Bucket or bag handles | Fingers hook under the handle; the palm may not wrap |
| Precision pinch | Small items (coin, key) | R4 — avoid unless essential |
| Lateral pinch | Cards, keys | R3–R4 |
| Palm support | Trays, plates | Open palm under the object; low risk |
| Two-hand grip | Heavy, wide, or fragile objects | Both hands must keep consistent contact |
| Cradle/hug | Large boxes, animals | Torso contact adds complexity |

## Approach orientation
- Align the palm and fingers to the handle's axis before contact: a horizontal handle is approached palm-down or palm-sideways; a vertical handle from the side.
- State the approach side when it matters (from above, from the side).
- Do not let the hand reverse orientation mid-grasp.

## Object choice and staging that lower risk
- Large, simple, rigid handles over small, thin, or ornate ones.
- Objects resting on a stable surface at a comfortable height.
- One hand over two when possible; two hands only when the object requires it.
- Medium framing over extreme macro; extreme close-ups of fingers need explicit testing.
- Sleeves, mittens, and gloves can reduce finger complexity, but only if consistent with the character design.
- Stylized characters with simplified hands (rounded, mitten-like) tend to be more stable; state the simplification in the model sheet.

## Grip confirmation signals
Before lift, verify at least two of:
- Fingers visibly wrap or rest on the correct part.
- Skin or fabric compresses at contact.
- A brief pause or tightening before lifting.
- The object tilts or shifts slightly when the hand takes load.
- Shadow changes under the object as it leaves the surface.

## Release signals
- Fingers open progressively; the hand withdraws.
- The object remains supported (on the surface or by another hand).
- No residual motion unless physical (rocking, swinging).

## Prompt patterns
- "Her right hand closes around the top handle of the watering can; her fingers wrap around the handle and her thumb rests on top."
- "He opens his left palm under the cup; the cup rests on his palm."
- "Her hand lets go; the can stays on the bench."
- Keep the contact sentence short, concrete, free of style adjectives.

## Common hand errors (see taxonomy B-group)
Finger count changes, fused or melting fingers, backward-bending joints, left/right swaps, an extra hand, scale drift. Fix by simplifying the pose, reframing, using a reference (subskill 04), or splitting the shot.

## Non-human grippers
For paws, tentacles, claws, robot grippers: define the contact surface and the grip mechanism ("the robot's two-finger gripper closes on the handle"). State digit counts. Keep one gripper action at a time.

## Checklist
- [ ] Finger count and left/right consistent
- [ ] Correct grip type for the object
- [ ] Palm orientation matches the handle before contact
- [ ] Grip confirmation visible before lift
- [ ] Clean release
- [ ] Framing exposes contact without extreme macro fingers
